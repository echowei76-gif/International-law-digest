ICJ Right to Strike Advisory Opinion and Trade–Labour Linkage (Systemic Integration)

On 21 May 2026 the ICJ delivered an advisory opinion on whether ILO Convention No. 87 protects the right to strike, a question referred by the ILO Governing Body after the Employers Group contested the point. The Court addressed the issue through treaty interpretation, including subsequent practice and interpretation in light of other relevant rules of international law. Commentary now asks what the opinion means for labour commitments in trade agreements and for the jurisdiction of trade adjudicators.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The opinion is a disciplined exercise in treaty interpretation: systemic integration under Article 31(3)(c) lets external rules inform the reading of existing commitments but does not create new obligations the parties never undertook. The Court's teleological reading confirms that strike action is a core tool of freedom of association, while the scope of commitments and the jurisdiction of adjudicative bodies remain governed by the relevant treaty. For trade–labour disputes, the opinion clarifies how labour norms may inform interpretation without displacing the bargain states struck.

Recognition of Palestine, Statelessness, and Refugee Law

A wave of recognitions of the State of Palestine has prompted legal analysis of what statehood recognition means for Palestinians who hold no other nationality. Under the international law of statelessness, recognition of Palestine may satisfy one condition for avoiding statelessness, but the analysis concludes that other conditions remain unmet, so Palestinians without another nationality should continue to be treated as stateless. The question has practical consequences for asylum and statelessness claims in Europe and elsewhere.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

Recognition is not per se constitutive of statehood, and the Montevideo criteria remain the benchmark; the legal status of an entity as a State is in principle independent of recognition. Recognition of Palestine is lawful and consistent with international law, and it confirms a broad international consensus grounded in the right to self-determination. At the same time, recognition does not automatically resolve the statelessness of Palestinians lacking another nationality, and refugee and statelessness determinations must proceed under the applicable instruments.

The recognitions reflect a strong international consensus based on international law and the inalienable right of the Palestinian people to self-determination, and the Security Council should recommend Palestine's admission as a full UN member. The persistence of statelessness is a consequence of occupation and of the failure to give full effect to statehood and self-determination, not a reason to deny protection. Refugee and statelessness frameworks should be read to ensure protection rather than to leave Palestinians in legal limbo.

Climate Justice, Coloniality, and Global South Development (Ranjitsinh and Decolonial Critique)

India's M K Ranjitsinh decision is being read as a turning point in comparative climate rights jurisprudence, showing that climate justice operates differently in the Global South, where climate change is intertwined with the right to development and energy equity. Critical scholarship frames the same terrain through 'climate coloniality,' arguing that climate impacts reproduce colonial and imperial patterns in postcolonial and occupied contexts. The debate concerns how constitutional and international law should balance mitigation ambition with developmental realities.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

Climate justice is best pursued through constitutionalism and rights-based regulation that expand the scope of regulatory mechanisms, advance renewable energy, and design localized responses. Equity in international climate law should reflect differentiated responsibility and vulnerability, with human rights frameworks building political will for greater ambition. Courts and regulators can integrate development concerns without abandoning mitigation commitments.

The Global South experiences disproportionate vulnerability despite minimal historical emissions, and climate policy framed around thresholds and net-zero targets can reproduce colonial patterns by constraining development. Climate coloniality describes how climate impacts and responses haunt postcolonial, occupied, and settler-colonial contexts, enrolling new spaces into coloniality. A decolonial humanist approach reclaims law's moral imagination and centers the knowledge and experience of those most affected, rather than treating development as an afterthought.

Investment Law and the Global South: South Centre Investment Case and ISDS Reform

UNCITRAL Working Group III continues its reform work on investor-State dispute settlement, including a draft statute for a permanent tribunal, while the EU promotes a multilateral investment court and other actors propose framework-convention approaches. The South Centre's 2026 investment case and report examine how joint interpretations of investment agreements affect the balance of power between states and arbitral tribunals. The debate concerns whether incremental reform can correct the system's perceived legitimacy deficits.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The system is undergoing modernization through multilateral processes: UNCITRAL's universal-membership working group is developing a permanent tribunal and procedural reforms, the EU has promoted a reformed approach since 2015 to ensure legitimacy, transparency, and neutrality, and transparency rules and mediation toolkits are already in place. Joint interpretations are a legitimate mechanism for states to assert control over treaty meaning, and the reform agenda is best advanced through consensus-based institution-building.

Joint interpretations are a defensive tool that can rebalance state–tribunal relations but depend on states' willingness to use them and on tribunals' deference. Proposals such as a framework convention on investment and sustainable development are needed to move beyond investor-protection-centric rules and to embed development and sustainability.
