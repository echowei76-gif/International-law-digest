Marine Geoengineering and International Legal Obligations

International law governing marine geoengineering is fragmented and underdeveloped, with the London Convention and London Protocol providing the only explicit regulation of activities such as ocean fertilization. The ITLOS advisory opinion clarified that UNCLOS Article 192 imposes a broad obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment, including from climate-related impacts, while environmental impact assessment requirements give effect to precaution and due diligence. Proposed marine geoengineering activities involve novel interventions, uncertain effects, and risks extending beyond national jurisdiction.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The existing framework — UNCLOS, the London Convention and Protocol, and customary rules on transboundary harm — provides a workable, precautionary basis for governing marine geoengineering through environmental impact assessments, due diligence, and prevention obligations. The priority is to strengthen and coordinate these regimes so that novel activities are carefully assessed and managed without foreclosing potentially necessary climate interventions.

Universal Jurisdiction and Atrocity Crimes Enforcement Gap

Universal jurisdiction allows states to prosecute core international crimes such as genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity, but enforcement remains uneven and politically contested. INTERPOL's 2010 framework for processing requests concerning these crimes contains limitations, including where a request concerns a national of a state, contributing to an enforcement gap. The African Union has criticized the concentration of universal jurisdiction cases brought by former colonial European states against individuals from the Global South, and has developed a model national law on universal jurisdiction.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

Universal jurisdiction is a legitimate and necessary tool to close impunity gaps for core international crimes, grounded in treaty obligations and customary rules requiring investigation and prosecution. The priority is to improve international cooperation, including through INTERPOL and national legislation, so that suspects cannot evade accountability, while addressing concerns about selectivity through procedural safeguards.

Academic Freedom and the Securitization of Universities

Universities in several countries face growing pressure on academic freedom, including government attacks on institutions such as Harvard and Columbia, security force incursions onto campuses, and political interference in research and teaching. Debates center on how to protect the university as a space for open inquiry while also addressing security concerns and internal disruptions to events and research formats. The tension is between the university as a site of democratic contestation and as a constitutionally protected space for scientific knowledge production.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

Academic freedom is a constitutional and structural principle tied to democratic governance and freedom of inquiry, protecting both institutional autonomy and individual scholars. The state and university leadership have a protective duty to keep the university open to provisional and dissenting theses, and security measures must not be used to suppress politically inconvenient research or teaching.
