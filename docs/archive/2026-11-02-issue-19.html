Rule-of-law backsliding and constitutional-court capture: the Polish Constitutional Tribunal crisis and its wider European implications

In late October 2026 a judge elected by parliament in March 2026, Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, ordered the suspension of Tribunal President Bogdan Święczkowski and Vice President Bartłomiej Sochański, prompting police deployment at the Tribunal, a crowd breaking the Tribunal gate, and the president cutting short a trip to hold crisis consultations. Former Tribunal presidents issued a statement calling for a path out of the crisis, the European Commission said parliament has the right to appoint judges and pointed to ECtHR interim measures of 5 May 2026, and the Commission's July 2026 Rule of Law Report recorded continuing problems with the Tribunal, execution of judgments and disputes over newly elected judges. Commentators also note contested appointments made by the Tusk government, including that of minister Maciej Berek, which polling suggested many Poles saw as politicising the judiciary.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The crisis is a rule-of-law problem in which a captured or delegitimised constitutional court blocks the execution of European judgments and undermines judicial independence; the remedy lies in restoring a court that meets European standards, with the Commission, ECtHR interim measures and the annual Rule of Law Report as the proper external anchors, and with parliament's appointment power treated as legitimate when exercised within that framework.

Climate/environmental justice through trade and investment law: the EU-NZ FTA climate complaint and France's fossil-fuel advertising ban

On 29 September 2026 the Dutch environmental justice organisation Both ENDS lodged the first environmental and climate complaint under the European Commission's Single Entry Point, alleging that New Zealand has broken the climate commitments in its FTA with the EU; the TSD chapter commits both sides not to lower environmental protections and retains their right to regulate. In parallel, France's partial ban on fossil-fuel advertising is being examined for its European implications, including proportionality, freedom of expression, internal market freedoms, the correct legal basis for EU action, and cross-border enforcement difficulties.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

Trade agreements can be made to serve environmental goals through enforceable sustainable-development chapters and civil-society complaints, and the EU-NZ complaint is a test of the EU's fidelity to the rule of international law and of the credibility of mutual commitments; the French ban is a legitimate public-interest measure whose extension requires careful calibration against expression and internal-market freedoms.

Collective enforcement of international judgments and accountability for Russia: Council of Europe proposals on ECtHR award payments

On 30 September 2026 PACE adopted Resolution 2678 (2026) and Recommendation 2315 (2026) endorsing, for the first time in Council of Europe history, concrete proposals to secure payment of ECtHR just-satisfaction awards against a state unwilling to pay, following Russia's exclusion from the Council of Europe in March 2022 and its later withdrawal from the ECHR and from the anti-torture convention. Related debate concerns whether frozen Russian central bank assets could be used to satisfy such awards, with questions raised about countermeasures, reversibility and collective enforcement under international law.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

Russia's non-payment and treaty withdrawals leave victims without remedies, and the PACE proposals are a proportionate, rule-of-law-preserving innovation that closes an enforcement gap while keeping the response within the framework of the Convention system and general international law.

Platform work, labour rights and the EU Platform Work Directive: 'employees without employers' and algorithmic management

The EU Platform Work Directive introduces a rebuttable presumption of employment where facts indicate control and direction by a digital labour platform, shifts the burden of proof to the platform, and imposes transparency and human-oversight requirements for automated monitoring and decision-making; member states face a December 2026 transposition deadline. Verfassungsblog notes that the presumption alone will not solve the problem, since delivery platforms have interposed 'fleet partners' between themselves and workers, and that algorithmic-management provisions apply to all platform workers regardless of employment status.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The Directive is a milestone that rebalances bargaining power through a rebuttable presumption, burden-shifting and algorithmic transparency, and the priority now is timely and consistent transposition across member states so that the level playing field and legal certainty the Directive promises are realised.

Global South / TWAIL

The presumption is easily circumvented by subcontracting chains and intermediary 'fleet partners', leaving workers formally employed by entities with no real employer function; without addressing fragmented employer structures and enforcement capacity, the Directive risks becoming a symbolic milestone rather than a substantive improvement in working conditions.
