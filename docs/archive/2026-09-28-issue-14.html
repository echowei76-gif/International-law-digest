Nicaragua v. Germany at the ICJ: Monetary Gold, Jurisdiction and Preliminary Objections

Nicaragua instituted proceedings against Germany before the ICJ in March 2024, alleging Germany's participation in plausible genocide and serious breaches of international humanitarian law in Gaza through military support to Israel. Germany filed preliminary objections to the Court's jurisdiction and admissibility in October 2025, and the ICJ held hearings on these objections from 7 to 10 September 2026. The case raises questions about the Monetary Gold principle, temporal limitations on Germany's acceptance of ICJ jurisdiction, and the scope of claims under the Genocide Convention.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The ICJ proceedings represent a proper invocation of the Court's jurisdiction under the Genocide Convention and the compulsory jurisdiction declarations of both states. Germany's preliminary objections raise legitimate legal questions about temporal limitations and the Monetary Gold principle, which the Court must resolve before reaching the merits. The case demonstrates the ICJ's role in adjudicating complex questions of state responsibility and the importance of procedural rigor in international dispute resolution.

The discussion revisits the ICJ's 2007 Bosnia v. Serbia judgment and its subsequent interpretation in Croatia v. Serbia, arguing that certain pronouncements have been wrongly perceived as construing the intent requirement narrowly. The debate also addresses the Convention's travaux préparatoires and the scope of protected groups.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The ICJ's jurisprudence has consistently interpreted the Genocide Convention's intent requirement in accordance with its object and purpose, requiring specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or in part. The Bosnia v. Serbia judgment established crucial precedents on state responsibility for genocide, including findings on systematic mistreatment and the need for conclusive evidence. Subsequent cases like Croatia v. Serbia have clarified and refined these standards, contributing to a coherent body of international law on genocide prevention and punishment.

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney welcomed the proposal, framing it as an alliance for democracies. The proposal builds on existing concepts of differentiated integration but would require treaty change and unanimous approval from all EU member states.

Mainstream / Institutionalist

The associate membership proposal represents an innovative response to geopolitical shifts, including US tariffs and threats to Canadian sovereignty, by offering a flexible framework for close cooperation without full EU membership. It builds on the EU's tradition of differentiated integration and could serve as a model for other like-minded democracies. The legal basis under Article 217 TFEU provides a pathway for association agreements, though the new status would require careful negotiation and ratification.
